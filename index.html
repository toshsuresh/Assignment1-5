03 / WRITE-UP

Write-up

Dataset, design rationale, interactions, development process, AI usage, and references. All values are daily weather observations, not forecasts or long-term climate trends.

Dataset & Question

How do temperature and precipitation vary across U.S. locations and seasons? The assignment-provided weather.csv contains 416,937 daily station records. The handout says the data comes from NOAA’s Global Historical Climatology Network daily (GHCNd) and was transformed by filtering out stations with only sparse measurements. This project focuses on daily minimum temperature (TMIN), maximum temperature (TMAX), and precipitation (PRCP) at stations in the 50 U.S. states. The file has no stations in D.C.

The cleaned subset has 409,042 records at 1,561 station locations, covering January 1–September 21, 2017. A station represents its measurement location, not an entire state. The two-station comparison keeps the question focused while letting readers choose their own locations.

Design Rationale

Position on a shared horizontal time axis makes timing and seasonality easy to compare. Temperature uses lines to reveal changes over time. Precipitation uses adjacent bars from a zero baseline to show distinct daily amounts. Both stations share the same vertical scale within each chart. Orange and teal stay consistent across charts, summaries, and details, and the B temperature line is dashed as a second identifier.

A map was considered, but it would emphasize location over seasonal change. A state-average view would obscure variation between stations and weight states unevenly as coverage changes. A temperature–precipitation scatterplot would make individual relationships visible but make the sequence of days harder to follow. Linked time series directly support the chosen question.

Implemented Interactions

  • Category I, dynamic queries: state, station, season, and date controls update both charts and every summary.
  • Category II, zooming: dragging across the overview strip selects a date interval and zooms both charts together. The full-period overview preserves context.
  • Category II, details on demand and highlighting: hovering or tapping either chart highlights the same date in both and reveals both stations’ readings. Arrow keys and the daily date picker offer another way to inspect observations.
  • Category II, encoding choice: switch between daily highs and lows. Reset controls restore the full view, and exporting saves the selected station records.

Data Cleaning & Limitations

We left the supplied weather.csv unchanged and built a cleaned copy from it. We kept only stations in the 50 U.S. states. This removed 7,895 records from Canadian provinces and from the U.S. territories of Guam, Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the Northern Mariana Islands. We removed extra spaces from station names, converted dates to a standard year-month-day format, and checked that coordinates and measurements were valid numbers, with no negative precipitation. Each station got an identifier based on its name, state, and coordinates. We checked for repeated station and date pairs and found none. In three records the daily low was higher than the daily high, so we blanked both temperatures for those days. Missing measurements stay blank, and the page never fills them in.

Units come from the weather.txt column descriptions supplied with the dataset. TMAX and TMIN are in degrees Fahrenheit and PRCP is in inches. We kept these units and did not convert any values.

Average temperature summaries are means of available daily highs or lows, not daily averages (TAVG). Precipitation totals include only observed days. Zero means an observed zero, while a missing reading remains unknown. Wet days have at least 0.01 inches. Lines break across missing dates or values, and no bar is drawn for a missing precipitation value. Coverage counts and daily details distinguish missing data from dry days. Charts rescale with the selection, and totals across unequal date ranges or coverage are not directly comparable.

January–February and September 1–21 are partial seasons, and the year ends early in this file. A single partial year cannot establish long-term climate trends.

Development Process & Team

We explored the supplied CSV, chose a focused two-station comparison, cleaned the data and checked the results, and connected the D3 charts to a shared set of station and date controls. Data validation, synchronized interactions, and responsive layout were the main areas of work.

Team members: Santosh Sureshkumar and Anitej Suklikar.

Contributions: We worked on and discussed the visualizations together. Santosh led data cleaning and built the D3 charts and the overview zoom strip. Anitej did the styling and wrote the write-up. We both worked on the controls and the page layout.

Total development time: about 16 people-hours. Together we worked about 4 hours per week for four weeks.

Most time-consuming work: keeping the linked interactions in sync. The date inputs, season presets, overview brush, hover cursor, and daily details all update one another, so we had to stop programmatic brush moves from re-triggering updates, snap brushed selections to whole days, and keep the inspected day inside the selected range. Debugging these feedback loops took more iterations than any other part of the project.

Design reflection: The biggest lesson was that the task should drive the encoding. Our question asks how two places change over the same days, so we put time on a shared horizontal axis, since position on a common scale is the most accurate channel for comparison. We also learned that the two measures need different scale choices. Temperature is an interval value, so its axis fits the data range instead of starting at zero, which keeps seasonal swings visible. Precipitation bars use length to compare amounts, so they always start at zero.

Working with real data showed us how much a table format can hide. A missing precipitation reading looks a lot like a dry day, so we kept missing values blank, broke lines at gaps, and added a coverage table so readers can tell the difference. We gave Station B a dashed line as a second cue beside color, and added reset buttons and a visible date range so users can always see and undo their current selection. We also showed the page to peers and got feedback. Reviewing the page against the rubric led us to rename the write-up sections to match it and to tighten the write-up text.
