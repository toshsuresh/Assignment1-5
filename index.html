The question & the dataset

How do temperature and precipitation vary across U.S. locations and seasons? The assignment-provided weather.csv contains 416,937 daily station records. The handout attributes the transformed dataset to NOAA’s Global Historical Climatology Network daily (GHCNd). This project focuses on daily minimum temperature (TMIN), maximum temperature (TMAX), and precipitation (PRCP) at stations in the 50 states and D.C.

The cleaned subset has 409,042 records at 1,561 station locations, covering January 1–September 21, 2017. A station represents its measurement location, not an entire state. The two-station comparison keeps the question focused while letting readers choose their own locations.

Why these charts?

Position on a shared horizontal time axis makes timing and seasonality easy to compare. Temperature uses lines to reveal changes over time; precipitation uses adjacent bars from a zero baseline to show distinct daily amounts. Both stations share the same vertical scale within each chart. Orange and teal stay consistent across charts, summaries, and details; the B temperature line is dashed as a second identifier.

A map was considered, but it would emphasize location over seasonal change. A state-average view would obscure variation between stations and weight states unevenly as coverage changes. A temperature–precipitation scatterplot would make individual relationships visible but make the sequence of days harder to follow. Linked time series directly support the chosen question.

Interactions, with a purpose

  • Category I — dynamic queries: state, station, season, and date controls update both charts and every summary.
  • Category I — brushing and linking: dragging the overview selects a date interval and zooms both charts together. The full-period overview preserves context.
  • Category II — details on demand and highlighting: hovering or tapping either chart highlights the same date in both and reveals both stations’ readings. Arrow keys and the daily date picker offer another way to inspect observations.
  • Category II — encoding choice: switch between daily highs and lows. Reset controls restore the full view; exporting saves the selected station records.

Cleaning & limitations

The reproducible Python script normalizes station names and dates, validates coordinates and numeric values, filters geographic scope, and creates station identifiers from name, state, and coordinates. It retains the first record for duplicate station/date keys and reports conflicts. No duplicates were found in this file. It excludes 7,895 records outside scope and blanks both temperature fields in three records where TMIN exceeds TMAX. Missing measurements remain blank; the application never imputes them.

No data dictionary was supplied. Temperature values appear to be Fahrenheit and precipitation values appear to be inches; these are inferred units, not independently verified metadata. The script leaves values in their supplied units. PRCP describes precipitation, including the liquid equivalent of snow, rather than rain alone. Raw NOAA files use different units, so this transformed CSV should not be interpreted using their native scaling.

Average temperature summaries are means of available daily highs or lows, not daily averages (TAVG). Precipitation totals include only observed days. Zero means an observed zero; a missing reading remains unknown. Wet days have at least 0.01 inches. Lines break across missing dates or values; no bar is drawn for a missing precipitation value. Coverage counts and daily details distinguish missing data from dry days. Charts rescale with the selection, and totals across unequal date ranges or coverage are not directly comparable.

January–February and September 1–21 are partial seasons, and the year ends early in this file. A single partial year cannot establish long-term climate trends. Station observation times and local conditions may also differ.
